A critique of Robert Pape's political-violence thesis

The symmetry
problem.

Political violence is real. But if the questions, definitions and datasets are different, saying “both sides” can obscure more than it explains. This investigation tests the evidence—not the slogan.

01 / The argument

A warning can be valid.
A comparison can still fail.

Pape's October 2026 University of Chicago report warns that large numbers of Americans express tolerance for political violence. That concern deserves attention. Yet the case for a symmetric left–right threat requires compatible measurements of actual behavior, equally worded survey questions, and clear legal definitions.

The central challenge: Are we comparing the same thing, measured the same way, in the same period?
Measurement

Different questions

“Force to remove Trump” and “military to stop protests” are not interchangeable survey items. Neither proves a person's willingness to commit an attack.

Behavior

Unequal incident rates

For 2025, CSIS reports sizable left–right differences in specified categories. That should be shown before making sweeping claims about symmetry.

Rule of law

Different kinds of force

A lawful removal order, an excessive-force incident, a peaceful demonstration, and a firebombing are not legally or morally interchangeable.

Test the distinctions ↗
Important fairness check: Pape did not need to claim equal numbers to be correct that violence on both sides is dangerous. This article challenges symmetry where it is suggested or inferred; it does not claim he explicitly said every form occurs equally often.

04 / The legal distinction

Not every use of force
is the same act.

The broad English word violence is not an all-purpose legal classification. Statutes authorize certain actions; the Constitution limits how the government executes them.

01Lawful coercion

Reasonable force during an authorized arrest or removal process, subject to the law and due process.

02Excessive force

Government action that fails the applicable constitutional or statutory standard.

03Political crime

Arson, threats, assaults, or attempted killings committed to advance political goals.

04Protected protest

Peaceful dissent, assembly and criticism, including sharp opposition to state policy.

05 / Election integrity • Alaska

Two Dan Sullivans.
One disputed scheme.

Pape referenced the dispute over similar candidate names. Reporting released the day of his episode revealed a considerably more deliberate operation than accidental name overlap. That matters—but it does not by itself establish that a court may invalidate the result.

Documented reporting

What the New York Times uncovered

Interviews, internal communications and documents indicated operatives aligned with Mary Peltola helped recruit candidates to split the incumbent Republican's vote, including another Dan Sullivan. Peltola's campaign and the DSCC denied involvement. A former campaign manager later sought to retract prior interviews; the Times said independent reporting supported the account.

NYT investigation ↗Alaska Public Media ↗
Unresolved legal question

Does deception mean certification can be blocked?

Not automatically. Alaska's Supreme Court allowed the second candidate on the ballot under existing qualification rules. Election-law experts question whether the DOJ's requested federal remedy is legally available. Recruiting spoilers may be ethically deceptive without automatically constituting a proven crime or vote-count fraud.

Ethically manipulative politics and legally provable election fraud are different claims. Condemn the former; prove the latter.
06 / Judicial legitimacy

Trust cannot be ordered.
It must be earned.

Pape's appeal to accept court rulings is a necessary democratic norm—but it cannot be the entirety of a plan for restoring legitimacy. Institutions need review, limits on power, disclosed interests, and fair procedures. That critique is stronger than declaring a majority of judges “corrupt” without case-specific evidence.

Estreicher & Girand / 2017–2025
93.6%

Share of nationwide injunctions issued by judges appointed by presidents of the opposing party, in these authors' study. The authors call nationwide injunctions a bipartisan problem.

Read the authors' analysis ↗

A real institutional remedy

Trump v. CASA (June 2025)

The Supreme Court limited nationwide injunctions that exceed what is required to give complete relief to plaintiffs. A 2026 Congressional Research Service report describes the legal limits and remaining exceptions. This is an example of a structural response to a procedural abuse concern—not a reason to disregard courts.

CRS analysis ↗

Judicial appointments ≠ corruption

A judge's appointing president, ideological background or adverse ruling is not proof of unlawful conduct. Even a strong statistical partisan pattern does not establish misconduct in individual cases.

Blackman's concrete objection

Josh Blackman criticized judges' withdrawals of planned senior status following Trump's election and inadequate inquiry into motives. His political interpretation is not a proven judicial-misconduct finding.

Read Blackman ↗

A bipartisan structural problem

Lawfare's national-injunction review documents judge-shopping across Obama, Biden and Trump presidencies. Any reform worth defending should apply consistently regardless of who occupies the White House.

Method & caveats ↗
Challenging a ruling through evidence, appeal, oversight and lawful reform is part of the rule of law, not a rejection of it.

08 / The remedies test

Will Pape's solutions
meet his diagnosis?

Pape proposes strong cross-party condemnation of violence, peaceful protest norms, trust in constitutional procedures, and policy changes to lower grievances. These may help—but no cited causal evaluation establishes they alone are sufficient to stop organized attacks or repair institutional legitimacy.

The Fourth Turning lens

A crisis of legitimacy is not a communications problem.

Neil Howe's Fourth Turning framework describes periodic crises of institutional legitimacy and renewal. It can sharpen the question Pape faces: if institutional incentives and performance are deteriorating, are calls for civility sufficiently structural? It does not prove violence is inevitable or forecast an exact date.

I. HighInstitutional confidence
II. AwakeningCultural challenge
III. UnravelingInstitutional fragmentation
IV. CrisisUrgency and possible renewal — the disputed present-day diagnosis
The serious objection

Pape's interventions may be necessary, not sufficient.

If distrust grows from incentives, enforcement inconsistencies, election procedures and institutional accountability failures, appeals to moderation will be fragile without legal and administrative changes.

The intellectual limit

Fourth Turning is a theory, not a proven law.

Reviewers question Howe's historical selection and predictive precision. A crisis narrative should not justify assuming political compromise is futile—or that a new conflict must occur.

10 / Editorial conclusion

Do not mistake universal
condemnation for symmetry.

A principled person should condemn both right-wing assassinations and left-wing arson. But that moral rule does not settle who is doing what, how frequently, under what definition, or during which year.

The test for Robert Pape is straightforward: same definitions, same intervals, same standards, and the legal distinction between coercion and crime.
Established

Recent asymmetries exist.

In specific 2025 CSIS categories, left-associated incidents outnumbered right-associated incidents. Pape's poll questions are not parallel.

Qualified

No timeless majority proved.

CSIS also documents considerably higher historical right-wing terrorism counts. Single-year shifts cannot establish all-time dominance.

Unresolved

Intent cannot be assumed.

Misleading framing is open to criticism; deliberate dishonesty or systemic judicial corruption requires independent proof.

Three questions for Pape

1. Why compare differently worded attitude measures?   2. How do survey answers map to completed attacks?   3. How does a civility campaign repair institutional incentives?
