Mental Illness Is Not a Moral Eraser

Lindsay Clancy, the insanity defense, and the line between explanation, excuse, and public safety.

Opinion / analysis · Updated September 6, 2026

Some criminal cases become difficult because we do not know what happened. The Lindsay Clancy case is difficult for almost the opposite reason. The central physical act is not seriously disputed. The dispute is over what the act means in law and morality: was this murder by a gravely ill but responsible person, or an act committed by someone whose mental disease had destroyed the capacity that makes criminal blame possible?

That question deserves more precision than either slogan usually receives. “She was mentally ill” is not enough. “She planned it, therefore she was sane” is not enough either.

The real question is not whether mental illness existed. It is whether the illness destroyed the particular capacities that make a person criminally responsible at the moment of the act.

Massachusetts law makes that distinction explicit. Once lack of criminal responsibility is properly raised, the Commonwealth must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was criminally responsible. A person is not criminally responsible if, because of a mental disease or defect, she lacked substantial capacity either to appreciate the wrongfulness or criminality of her conduct or to conform her conduct to the requirements of law.

That is a demanding and morally serious standard. But precisely because the consequences are so large, we should ask whether the evidence actually meets it rather than treating a diagnosis—or our sympathy—as the answer.

Status of the case: On September 4, 2026, Judge William Sullivan declared a mistrial after the jury remained deadlocked following a nearly six-week trial. Clancy remains charged; prosecutors have not yet announced whether they will retry the case. This essay argues a position about the evidence and the insanity doctrine. It does not describe Clancy as legally guilty, because no final verdict has established that.

Part I: The strongest case against the insanity defense in Clancy

1. The contemporaneous record matters more than the retrospective story

Clancy unquestionably had serious psychiatric problems. But severe depression, anxiety, insomnia, suicidal thinking and intrusive thoughts are not synonymous with psychosis, and psychosis itself is not automatically legal insanity.

One of the hardest facts for the defense is that people were professionally evaluating her close to the killings. Two psychiatrists who treated her before the deaths testified that they did not observe psychosis. Dr. Jennifer Tufts, who saw Clancy the day before the killings, testified that Clancy denied suicidal and homicidal ideation and that Tufts saw no signs of psychosis or mania. Dr. Alia Goodheart likewise testified that during an inpatient stay earlier that month she did not see signs leading her to fear Clancy was a danger to herself or others.

This does not prove that psychosis was impossible. Psychiatric symptoms can fluctuate. Patients conceal symptoms. Telehealth and brief clinical encounters can miss things. But when a later legal defense depends heavily on an extreme psychotic state, the absence of clear contemporaneous documentation is evidence—not trivia.

There is an important epistemic principle here: the closer the observation is to the event, and the less incentive the observer or patient has to shape a legal narrative, the more weight it generally deserves.

2. The alleged psychosis must explain the behavior better than the non-psychotic alternative

The prosecution did not have to prove that Clancy was mentally healthy. Its stronger theory was narrower: she could have been profoundly depressed and suicidal while still retaining criminal responsibility.

Prosecution psychiatrist Dr. Avram Mack said he found evidence of a major depressive episode but not mania or psychosis and concluded that Clancy could understand that what she was doing was wrong. Forensic psychologist Dr. Kirk Heilbrun also challenged the account of a short-lived command hallucination, describing an isolated voice appearing only during the critical episode as highly unusual and raising the possibility that the hallucination account was exaggerated or fabricated.

The defense presented the opposite expert conclusion. Dr. Phillip Resnick testified that Clancy was frankly psychotic and experienced a command hallucination and a delusion of external control. That conflict is exactly why this is not a simple case. But when experts disagree, the jury is entitled to return to behavior, contemporaneous records, timing, consistency and corroboration.

3. Planning is not proof of sanity—but it is evidence of retained capacity

A common anti-insanity argument says: she waited until her husband left, therefore she was sane. That is too simplistic. Psychotic people can plan. Andrea Yates famously carried out a highly organized sequence of killings and was ultimately found not guilty by reason of insanity.

But the opposite claim—that planning tells us nothing—is also wrong.

The prosecution argued that Clancy arranged a window in which Patrick Clancy would be away getting food and medication, then acted while she was alone with the children. It characterized the killings as deliberate and premeditated. Organized sequencing, timing behavior around another person’s absence, choosing methods and locations, and adapting conduct to circumstances are all relevant to whether a person retained substantial behavioral control.

Massachusetts itself has an instructive precedent. In Commonwealth v. McDermott, a defendant who killed seven coworkers raised an insanity defense. A prosecution expert relied in part on organized conduct—test-firing weapons privately, secretly bringing them into the workplace, functioning normally beforehand, and responding rationally when police arrived—to conclude that the defendant appreciated wrongfulness and could conform his conduct. The jury rejected the insanity defense, and the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court later discussed that reasoning.

The point is not that organization equals sanity. The point is that behavior demonstrating delay, concealment, sequencing and adaptation is evidence bearing on the very capacities the insanity test asks about.

4. The timing of “postpartum psychosis” deserves scrutiny, not dismissal

Postpartum psychosis is real, rare and dangerous. A 2026 expert consensus review describes it as a severe psychiatric illness strongly connected to the bipolar spectrum. Most cases begin rapidly after childbirth: prospective studies place the majority within the first two weeks, and epidemiologic research generally places onset within the first twelve weeks postpartum.

Clancy’s youngest child was about eight months old at the time of the killings. That does not make a postpartum connection impossible, and it certainly does not prove that she was not psychotic. But it makes a newly appearing, childbirth-triggered psychosis less typical than the classic presentation. If the defense uses the postpartum label as causal explanation, temporal fit is a legitimate evidentiary question.

The intellectually responsible skeptical position is therefore not “postpartum psychosis is fake.” It is: a real disorder still has diagnostic features, timing patterns and evidentiary requirements.

5. Mental illness is an explanation; the law still has to decide whether it is an excuse

This is the conceptual point around which everything else turns.

A person with bipolar disorder can commit murder while manic and remain criminally responsible. Another person with bipolar disorder could, in an extreme psychotic episode, meet the legal insanity standard. The diagnosis does not decide the case.

The same is true of schizophrenia, severe depression, PTSD, personality disorders and other conditions. Psychiatry describes disorders. Criminal law asks a narrower functional question: what capacities did this defendant retain at the exact time of the act?

That distinction protects mentally ill defendants from unjust punishment—but it also protects society from turning every psychiatric explanation into an exemption from responsibility.

Part II: The deeper philosophical problem

Explanation cannot be allowed to swallow responsibility

Every human act has causes. Genes influence temperament. Childhood affects attachment. Trauma changes threat perception. Brain injury alters inhibition. Hormones influence mood. Poverty changes incentives. Addiction changes desire. Mental illness can profoundly change thought.

If discovering a cause automatically erased responsibility, responsibility itself would disappear.

A civilized theory of responsibility cannot ask whether behavior was caused. All behavior is caused. It has to ask which kinds and degrees of impairment are sufficient to suspend ordinary moral agency.

This is why insanity law needs a threshold rather than a diagnosis list. The morally relevant issue is whether the defendant still possessed enough reality testing, moral understanding and behavioral control to be treated as an agent rather than merely as the location where an event occurred.

The continuum problem

There is no magical biological line between “healthy brain” and “diseased brain.” Human capacities exist on continua.

If overwhelming psychosis excuses because it destroys control, what about severe mania? Extreme PTSD? Intellectual disability? Compulsive disorders? Severe sleep deprivation? Brain tumors? Dementia? Rage? Addiction? Intoxication?

The answer cannot simply be “whatever psychiatry diagnoses,” because diagnostic categories were built primarily for clinical treatment and research, not for assigning moral desert. Nor can the answer be “nothing ever excuses,” because a person who literally cannot comprehend reality is not situated like an ordinary intentional offender.

The best principle is functional and demanding: an excuse should require evidence that the impairment destroyed a core capacity necessary for responsibility, not merely that it influenced the decision.

Sympathy must not change the threshold

The insanity standard should apply the same way to defendants we instinctively sympathize with and defendants we fear or dislike.

A devoted mother with postpartum illness can evoke enormous empathy. A homeless man with schizophrenia screaming threats on a subway may evoke fear. A bipolar man in a manic state may evoke neither. But if the legal test changes depending on how culturally sympathetic the defendant appears, it is no longer a legal principle.

Equal justice means the same question for all of them: what mental disease existed, what capacities did it actually destroy, and what evidence proves that destruction?

The Daniel Penny/Jordan Neely subway case is sometimes pulled into this debate, but it is not an insanity precedent. Penny was tried for his own conduct in restraining Neely and was acquitted of criminally negligent homicide in December 2024 after the manslaughter count was dismissed when jurors deadlocked. Neely’s mental illness was part of the factual background and public-safety debate, not a successful insanity defense for a homicide he committed in that encounter.

Culpability and dangerousness are different questions

A frequent fear about insanity acquittals is: if someone was “not responsible” for killing, does society simply let the person walk out the door?

That is not how the law has to work, and Massachusetts law specifically provides a route for hospitalization and continued commitment after a verdict of not guilty by reason of mental illness or defect. Commitment proceedings focus on mental illness and the likelihood of serious harm, with periodic review.

The U.S. Supreme Court recognized this distinction in Jones v. United States: an insanity acquittee may be confined for treatment and protection of society while mental illness and dangerousness justify confinement, even though the person was not criminally convicted.

This yields an important philosophical separation:

Punishment asks: how blameworthy was the past act?

Incapacitation asks: how dangerous is the person now?

Treatment asks: what intervention may reduce that danger and restore capacity?

A coherent system can answer those questions differently. Someone might deserve less punishment because of genuine insanity yet still require very restrictive confinement because the risk of another catastrophic act remains high.

If a condition can erase responsibility, it can matter to prospective safety—but only individually

There is a provocative argument sometimes raised around postpartum psychosis: if a biological condition can unpredictably destroy judgment badly enough to excuse homicide, why should that condition be considered irrelevant in safety-critical professions?

There is a logically valid point hidden inside an overbroad conclusion.

If a particular person is experiencing a condition capable of severely impairing judgment, that condition can rationally matter to whether she should temporarily fly a commercial aircraft, perform surgery or control other high-risk systems. We already accept individualized fitness restrictions for epilepsy, acute mania, substance impairment and other conditions.

What does not follow is that an entire sex should be treated as unreliable because a rare subset can experience a temporary disorder. Risk management should follow the condition and the individual, not stereotype the class.

The danger of retrospective reconstruction

Insanity cases have a special evidentiary problem: the legal system is trying to reconstruct a private mental state after an extraordinary event.

The defendant survived. The victims often did not. Experts may interview the defendant months later. Memory has changed. Lawyers have developed theories. Medical terminology has been introduced. The person now knows what happened and what is legally at stake.

None of that means defendants are lying. It means retrospective claims should be checked aggressively against information created before the legal incentive existed: medical notes, messages, searches, observed behavior, statements made immediately afterward, prior symptoms, and the structure of the act itself.

This is not hostility toward psychiatry. It is ordinary evidentiary discipline.

Insanity defenses are uncommon—which is an argument for precision, not complacency

The public sometimes imagines the insanity defense as a routine loophole. Classic multi-state research found it was raised in roughly one percent of felony cases and succeeded in only about a quarter of those cases. Those figures are old and jurisdiction-specific, so they should not be treated as a current national statistic. But they do show why slogans about criminals routinely “getting off on insanity” are misleading.

That rarity, however, does not answer whether the doctrine is correctly applied in any particular homicide. A rare legal doctrine can still be too broad in one case or too narrow in another.

A better rule of thought

The best skeptical position on insanity is not cruelty toward mentally ill people. And the best defense of insanity is not that mental illness makes agency disappear whenever psychiatry can explain an act.

A principled system should insist on five things:

First: diagnosis alone never decides responsibility.

Second: the claimed impairment must be connected specifically to the legally relevant capacities at the time of the offense.

Third: contemporaneous and behavioral evidence should receive great weight when retrospective expert accounts conflict.

Fourth: the threshold must be applied equally across sympathetic and unsympathetic defendants, across sex, diagnosis and social status.

Fifth: an insanity finding should not be confused with a public-safety finding. A person may be non-punishable yet still require confinement while dangerous.

Where that leaves Lindsay Clancy

The Clancy case should not become a referendum on whether postpartum psychosis exists. It does. Nor should it become a referendum on whether mothers deserve compassion. They do.

The narrower and harder question is whether Lindsay Clancy’s illness actually deprived her of the substantial capacity Massachusetts law requires for criminal responsibility during the killings.

The skeptical evidence is meaningful: clinicians close to the event did not document psychosis; prosecution experts found severe depression without the necessary loss of responsibility; the reported command hallucination was disputed; the conduct showed organization and timing; and the postpartum timing was atypical for classic new-onset postpartum psychosis.

The defense evidence is also meaningful: she had plainly deteriorated psychiatrically, had sought substantial help, attempted suicide immediately after the killings, and defense experts concluded that she was psychotic. Eleven jurors reportedly favored a non-responsibility verdict, though the jury as a legal body reached no verdict at all.

That is why the intellectually serious position is not certainty.

Compassion should affect how carefully we look. It should not decide what we see.

Mental illness can diminish responsibility. At its most extreme, it can destroy responsibility. But because the difference between explanation and excuse determines both justice for the accused and justice for the dead, the burden of drawing that line should remain exacting.
