# Vision — The Shortcut: The Moon Bell

## North-star experience

A player should feel they discovered a **lost early-1990s Sierra fairy-tale adventure that has been beautifully remastered for a modern browser**.

Not a workplace simulation. Not an RPG. Not a dialogue questionnaire. Not a puzzle-room collection.

The player walks through an enchanted kingdom, observes how places and creatures behave, carries a small inventory, talks to memorable characters, learns magical rules, notices timed opportunities, revisits locations after dusk, and solves problems in more than one way.

## Fantasy promise

You are **Mara Vale**, a young locksmith's apprentice in Ardenfall.

A storm has washed out the royal road. The injured Bellkeeper entrusts you with the **Sun Key** and one instruction:

> Get it to Castle Arden before the ninth evening bell.

The obvious road is gone. The only remaining route is the abandoned **Briar Road** through an old enchanted wood.

The deeper Mara goes, the more she learns: the Sun Key does not merely open a tower. It releases the yoke of the ancient Moon Bell, whose ringing renews a fragile compact between the Crown and Briarwood. At moonrise, the wood begins to wake.

The title **The Shortcut** refers both to the road and to the player's repeated opportunities to save time by borrowing, trespassing, tricking, waiting, helping, or finding another way.

## Pillars

### 1. Fairy-tale exploration
Distinct authored locations with strong silhouettes, visual storytelling, readable paths, magical details, and environmental surprises.

### 2. Adventure-game reasoning
The player observes, remembers, experiments, combines items, uses people and places at the right time, and reaches "oh!" moments.

### 3. Living schedules
A few NPC routines materially change opportunities. The player can learn them from the world rather than a schedule screen.

### 4. A changing world
Late afternoon becomes dusk, then moonlit night. New paths appear; old routes close; creatures wake; flowers open; the castle changes.

### 5. Alternate solutions
Several major obstacles support at least two valid approaches. Faster is not always safer; generous is not always fastest; clever shortcuts can help or complicate later scenes.

### 6. Consequences without moral scoring
The game remembers permission, promises, theft, help, witnesses, and favors, but never labels the player good or evil.

### 7. Beautiful authored composition
The environment is the star. Characters are small but expressive, like classic Sierra staging.

## Player verbs
Walk, look, talk, take, give, use, open, close, pull, turn, place, pour, light, wait, listen, hide, knock, ring.

No software/workplace verbs.

## Desired player memories
After finishing, a player should remember:
- the flooded mill and the impossible-looking crossing;
- the forest signs that will not stay put;
- Ysabet's crooked cottage and the temptation of her unattended lantern;
- the moonwell changing after dusk;
- Brindle asleep beneath his mossy bridge;
- the castle silhouette under a rising moon;
- at least one puzzle they solved by inference rather than by exhausting buttons;
- one moment where an earlier choice clearly changed a later scene.

If the player mainly remembers menus, objectives, or dialogue lists, the design failed.
