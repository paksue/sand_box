HUNGER What are you still hungry for?

The food is only the surface. Underneath it is a movie about class, prestige, resentment, attention, love—and the moment ambition stops serving you and starts eating you alive.

What it is really about

Not food. Not even ambition. It is about the manufacture of desire.

Hunger starts as the familiar story of a gifted outsider entering an elite world. But the movie keeps changing the question. At first it seems to ask, “Can Aoy become a great chef?” By the end, the better question is, “Who taught her what greatness is supposed to look like?”

Food is the perfect vehicle because it can be necessity, pleasure, memory, art, status symbol and weapon—all at once. A bowl of noodles can keep someone alive. The exact same human need can also become a stage on which wealthy people display how far above necessity they live.

Director Sitisiri Mongkolsiri has said the movie uses food as a metaphor for hungers that remain after the stomach is full: acceptance, fame, love, recognition and more. That is the organizing principle of the film. Every major character is hungry. The tragedy is that most of them confuse having an appetite with knowing what will satisfy it.

Filmmaker intentNetflix’s production notes quote Mongkolsiri describing the story as two people driven by their respective “hunger,” and food as a metaphor for desires beyond eating. The movie’s class politics are therefore not a side reading; they are built into the premise.
The dangerous hunger is not wanting more. It is needing somebody else to confirm that what you have is worth wanting.Central reading
Chef Paul

Paul is not simply Aoy’s villain. He is her possible future.

This is the key that makes Paul’s exaggerated behavior work. In an interview, Mongkolsiri described Paul as deliberately heightened rather than strictly realistic: a fantasy-like character who represents the dark side of Aoy. Once you know that, their relationship stops being merely master and student. It becomes a person arguing with the version of herself she might become.

Both begin with humiliation. Aoy is tired of being ordinary. Paul remembers being beneath people who could assign absurd value to a jar of caviar while treating his mother’s labor as disposable. The caviar story explains not only his anger but his entire business model.

Paul does not respond to class hierarchy by rejecting it. He studies it, masters it, and reverses the direction of humiliation. His ambition is essentially: if the rich decide what has value, I will become so valuable that the rich have to beg for me.

That is why his success feels triumphant and poisoned at the same time. He has defeated his childhood powerlessness, but only by accepting the original premise—that value is created by prestige, scarcity and domination.

Paul correctly diagnoses the game. His tragedy is deciding that the only answer is to become its best player.
The title

Everyone is hungry. They simply hunger for different currencies.

Aoy

To stop being ordinary. Skill matters, but recognition becomes the proof that the skill is real.

Paul

To control the class that once controlled him. Prestige becomes revenge refined into a profession.

Tone

To have talent validated on his own terms. Friendship becomes harder once Aoy outruns him.

Tos

To manufacture the next cultural asset. He understands chefs as brands before Aoy understands herself as one.

The clients

To feel exclusive. Once luxury becomes normal, the experience must grow rarer, stranger and more forbidden.

The audience

To decide whether we are watching aspiration, exploitation, or both—and why the spectacle remains seductive.

The smartest part of the title is that hunger is not automatically noble or corrupt. Hunger gets people out of bed. It makes Aoy practice until she can control a brutal wok and execute Paul’s standards. But hunger has no built-in stopping condition. Without a definition of “enough,” achievement only teaches the appetite to demand a larger meal.

Flesh and blood

Why do the rich diners suddenly look like animals?

Because the film wants sophistication to collapse in front of us. The room says refinement: private event, rare ingredients, elite guests, choreographed service. Then the diners tear into meat while red sauce runs down their faces. The social costume disappears and appetite becomes physical again.

Mongkolsiri has said that unease was intentional. He wanted food to look beautiful and a little disgusting at the same time, and he directed the eating scenes toward the primitive condition shared by rich and poor: when hunger takes over, manners can vanish.

But the images do more than say “everyone is an animal.” They make consumption look predatory. The wealthy guests are not merely eating meat; the movie frames them as people whose social position allows them to consume labor, rarity, attention, spectacle and other people’s obedience along with dinner.

The pool party is the same argument, made louder.

Food sits beside alcohol, sex, bodies, money and performance. Paul’s cooking is no longer nourishment. It is one sensation in a sequence of sensations. That matters because it shows what happens when luxury itself becomes ordinary: the customer needs a stronger hit. The appetite migrates from good to rare, then from rare to forbidden.

The “combat” structure

The movie keeps staging fights because cooking is only the visible battlefield.

You noticed the combat quality correctly. Hunger repeatedly converts culinary work into duels, trials, humiliations and showdowns. Each one is actually fighting over a different definition of value.

Audition
Aoy vs. the culinary-school candidate

Lived skill vs. credential. Paul appears meritocratic: he recognizes the wok cook whom the elite system would overlook. But the scene plants a later irony—Aoy still needs a powerful gatekeeper to declare that her ability counts.

Wagyu ordeal
Aoy vs. Paul’s standard

Potential vs. discipline. Paul is abusive, but the skill gap is real. Aoy’s overnight practice matters because the film refuses an easy moral escape: a cruel teacher can still teach something valuable.

Kitchen sabotage
Dang vs. the younger chefs

Scarcity vs. solidarity. The hierarchy teaches workers to experience another person’s advancement as their own disappearance. The fight occurs below the people who created the conditions.

Career divergence
Aoy vs. Tone

Friendship vs. ranking. Tone can admire Aoy when he is the discoverer and mentor. Her rise becomes harder to metabolize when it changes the relative status between them.

Final banquet
Aoy vs. Paul

Cooking vs. attention. The climactic duel reveals that the scarce resource in the room is not flavor. It is the crowd’s attention—and Paul already owns it before the first spoon is lifted.

Three layers of combatThe visible fight is chef vs. chef. The social fight is outsider vs. establishment, worker vs. boss, poor vs. rich. The deepest fight is each person vs. the hunger inside them.
Scenes that do more than they seem

The film’s smaller scenes keep testing the same question: who gets to possess value?

The chef who steals expensive ingredients

A worker can spend every day touching luxury without ever being allowed to own it. That is why the theft matters. Proximity to wealth is not participation in wealth. Paul’s response is pure system logic—rules were broken, so the worker is removed—but the scene quietly asks what kind of workplace trains people to prepare abundance they are never meant to share.

The indebted family and the murder-suicide

This is one of the film’s bluntest but most useful contrasts. The family can still purchase the appearance of prosperity while their underlying life is collapsing. The private meal becomes an epitaph for the equation “successful-looking = flourishing.” Near the same stretch of the story, Aoy’s own family is dealing with a real health crisis. One household spends to preserve status; another needs resources for survival.

Crybaby Noodles in the hospital

Aoy’s family dish threatens Paul more than another ambitious chef does. If inexpensive food made inside ordinary relationships can carry profound value, then Paul has sacrificed his life to dominate a scoreboard that may not measure the thing he actually needed. His dismissal of “food made with love” reads less like strength than self-protection.

Paul’s economy

Value is scarcity.

Rarity, reputation, exclusivity and the ability to deny access create desire. The chef becomes more valuable when fewer people can reach him.

Aoy’s home

Value is continuity.

A dish matters because people have eaten it together, remember it, return to it, and care about the person who made it even when nobody is watching.

The point of no return

The hornbill is not about cuisine. It is about the pleasure of exemption.

The protected bird scene is where the movie stops allowing Paul’s philosophy to hide behind culinary excellence. The client does not need a hornbill because it is the world’s most delicious ingredient. The forbiddenness is part of the product.

The meal says: ordinary people are constrained by laws; I can purchase an experience that proves those laws do not fully apply to me. In interviews, Mongkolsiri has explicitly connected the film’s class and political concerns to this world of power, status and elite impunity.

Paul’s participation completes his transformation. The boy who hated seeing wealth overpower his mother has become the professional who converts somebody else’s power into an exquisite plate.

He wanted to make the powerful kneel for his food. Instead, he became the craftsman who beautified their power.
The second mentor

Tos is more polite than Paul—and potentially more dangerous.

Paul’s exploitation is easy to see because it comes with shouting, humiliation and impossible standards. Tos works through encouragement. He tells Aoy she is special, gives her resources, creates Flame around her image, and helps convert her ability into fame.

That sounds better because in many ways it is better. But Tos understands the cultural machine in a way Aoy does not yet understand herself. Paul commodifies food. Tos commodifies Aoy: her rise, her rivalry, her authenticity, her story.

The final revelation—that strings were pulled and Tone’s footage became part of the transaction—forces Aoy to see that her supposed autonomous triumph has been engineered. Her ambition made her vulnerable to two opposite techniques of control: Paul said, “You are not enough.” Tos said, “You are the next big thing.” Both statements can make another person easier to direct.

The last service

The final cook-off reveals that Paul’s true product was never food.

The banquet is the entire movie compressed into one sequence. Aoy and Paul begin by competing through culinary spectacle. Each tries to seize the room. Fire becomes theater. The giant hanging carcass takes Aoy’s language of heat and enlarges it into something grotesquely theatrical.

Aoy eventually returns to Crybaby Noodles—the food tied to family rather than prestige. For a moment it seems the movie will hand us the easy victory: authenticity defeats pretension.

Then Paul serves something ordinary, and the room moves toward him anyway.

That is his final lesson. He is not saying, “My soup tastes better.” He is saying, “I am Chef Paul, therefore whatever I serve arrives already desired.” Reputation acts before taste. The guests have crowned him in advance.

Why the scene mattersAt the beginning, Paul functions as the person who can recognize Aoy’s talent. At the end, he demonstrates the darker side of that same power: recognized authority can shape what other people experience as valuable.

The giant cow is not excess for its own sake.

It is the logic of an attention economy made physical. If your measure of success is “make them look at me,” there is always a larger flame, a rarer ingredient, a more outrageous reveal, a more exclusive room. Spectacle has no natural finish line.

The ending

Paul is arrested. Aoy still does not feel like she won.

That emotional mismatch is the point. If this were simply a story about defeating an evil chef, Paul’s downfall would release Aoy into triumph. Instead the victory feels contaminated. Tone traded the video. Tos shaped the opportunity. The party itself immediately moves on to its next entertainment.

Aoy sees the machinery behind the applause. She has finally received the external validation she wanted and, at the exact moment of receiving it, discovers that validation can be manufactured, traded and redirected.

Her breakthrough is not realizing that ambition is bad. It is realizing that ambition needs a definition of “enough.”

Why going home is not surrender

A weaker version of this ending would say: “Elite cooking is corrupt; go back to the humble life you had before.” That is not what Aoy does. She returns with more discipline, knowledge, technique, confidence and agency. She intends to make a menu and be the chef.

So the ending is not reject growth. It is keep the skill; change the scoreboard.

The family restaurant looked small to Aoy because she had been measuring it using a prestige system that considered it small. The building has barely changed. Aoy’s understanding of value has.

Paul has countless admirers and almost no intimacy. Aoy can lose a room full of elite attention and still return to people who care whether she exists when she is not impressive. The film puts those two forms of wealth beside one another and asks which one is actually scarce.

Where it is weaker

The film sometimes explains its metaphor after the image has already done the work.

Paul can feel less like a person than a philosophical instrument. His speeches are effective, but occasionally the screenplay makes him say what the cinematography has already communicated. The wealthy clients are often so grotesque that they stop being people and become an illustrated argument.

The career trajectories are accelerated to the point of fantasy. Tone’s resentment, Tos’s manipulation, and the later professional reversals could all have used more time. The indebted-family tragedy is thematically precise but structurally feels inserted to underline a point the movie has already established.

Yet some of this exaggeration is intentional. Mongkolsiri has described Paul as heightened and fantasy-like, and his horror background is visible in the way dining bodies become grotesque, meat becomes spectacle and elite spaces feel faintly unreal.

So the question is less “Is this how every Michelin kitchen behaves?” and more “Does the exaggeration reveal something true?” Often, yes.

Final verdict

A blunt film with a sharp central idea.

8.5
out of 10

Hunger is strongest when it lets cooking become a language for class and desire rather than merely a plot about professional success.

Its symbolism can be heavy-handed. Its rich people can become cartoons. Its timeline bends credibility. But it builds a coherent argument from beginning to end: prestige can tell you what to want, but it cannot tell you when you have enough.

Paul sees the hierarchy and decides to conquer it. Aoy nearly does the same. Her real victory is subtler than beating him at a banquet. She becomes capable of playing Paul’s game and decides she no longer wants that game to define the score.

The opposite of hunger is not having everything. It is knowing what is enough.Final reading
