First, there was a village.

Ten households. A bakery. One hundred loaves of bread. One thousand coins. No stock market, central bank, or economic jargon—just people making things for one another.

100 loaves ÷ 10 households = 10 loaves per household, at a price of 10 coins each.

A challenge to the player

What the model reveals.

Technology can cut the unit cost of a good; when output rises faster than the money spent on it, its money price can fall. New money changes the price story. Who receives it first changes relative purchasing power, while who owns productive assets changes claims on the surplus.

Try this: make four inventions, keep money constant, then issue 3,000 new coins while giving all of them to the two owners. Watch Maya, Leo, and Nora.

The distribution experiment illustrates a Cantillon-type effect: injection channels matter. It is not a literal model of Federal Reserve operations.

Intellectual honesty mode

What it does not prove.

  • Not all products approach a zero price: land, human labor, scarcity, regulation, demand and resource constraints can push prices up.
  • Inflation is not solely explained by money creation. Supply shocks, spending velocity, credit, production, market power and policy matter.
  • Price deflation can improve affordability, yet debts fixed in nominal units and recessions introduce real trade-offs.
  • A negative result in gold is not the standard CPI meaning of a negative real investment return. Ignoring dividends distorts comparisons.
Methods & references

Trace the evidence yourself.

Two separate worlds: the fictional village uses the stripped-down identity price = circulating money / output, holding spending turnover fixed. The market desk uses published historical input values. The toggle marked * for total returns uses a third-party calculated approximation based on dividend reinvestment; its starting investment timing is not perfectly identical to the annual-average price-index illustration. The tool is educational, not a forecast or accusation about particular people.
