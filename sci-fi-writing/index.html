Shortest useful definition: Great science fiction asks: if this impossible-or-not-yet-real thing became real, what would it do to people, institutions, relationships, and meaning?

Core manual

What great science fiction actually needs.

The strongest work usually shares the same architecture: a powerful change to reality, clear rules, believable consequences, a human being under pressure, and a final choice that cannot be solved by gadgets alone.

Big Idea × Consequences × Human Nature × Story = Compelling Science Fiction
Change

One dangerous “what if?”

The premise should be easy to say in one sentence and powerful enough to rearrange incentives, identity, power, or survival.

  • What changed?
  • Why does it matter?
  • Who instantly benefits?
Rules

Limitations create drama

Unlimited powers kill tension. Decide what the speculative element can do, cannot do, costs, breaks, and who controls it.

  • Access
  • Cost
  • Failure modes
Human

Put a life in the blast radius

A world is not yet a story. Choose a protagonist whose desire collides violently with the new system.

  • What do they want?
  • Why are they vulnerable?
  • What choice will break them?
Build sequence

The seven-stage creation path.

Use this sequence when developing your own story. It prevents beautiful-but-empty worldbuilding and keeps the narrative moving toward human stakes.

01

Find the “What if?”

Good premises alter scarcity, power, identity, or survival. Ask whether your idea has a large consequence radius.

CapabilityDiscoveryConstraintInstitutionBiology
02

Give the idea rules

What are the access rules, costs, limits, control structures, failure modes, and black-market exploits?

  • If everyone gets it, what inequality survives?
  • If only elites get it, what resistance appears?
  • If it solves one problem, what dependency does it create?
03

Run the consequence machine

Think second- and third-order. Go beyond the invention itself and ask what people do because it exists.

  • Family
  • Work and money
  • Crime and law
  • Religion and status
  • Love and trust
04

Choose the human at the center

Pick the person most damaged, tempted, cornered, or morally divided by the system.

Character = Desire + Vulnerability + Pressure.
05

Create conflict from the premise

If the speculative element disappeared, your plot should collapse. The premise must generate the obstacle, not decorate it.

  • What do they want right now?
  • How does the new reality block it?
  • What harder choice does that force next?
06

Drive toward the impossible choice

The ending should not hinge on a technical trick alone. It should reveal what the story has really been arguing about.

Safety vs freedomLove vs truthIdentity vs utilitySurvival vs dignity
07

Leave a philosophical afterimage

Great science fiction lingers because the plot ends but the human question remains. Ask what the story is secretly investigating.

What counts as human?Should every power be used?Is a copy still a person?What is worth sacrificing for order?Can technology redeem loneliness?
Consequence thinking

Push the world past the obvious.

Most shallow science fiction stops at the gadget. Stronger work follows the ripple effects. Write one consequence, then ask “and because of that?” five times.

Consequence domains

Family
Who becomes a parent, child, spouse, or burden differently?
Money
What becomes valuable? What disappears?
Law
What becomes provable, illegal, or impossible to enforce?
Crime
What new criminal market appears?
Love
How do trust, jealousy, and intimacy mutate?

Premise examples

  • Everyone knows their death date. Insurance, marriage, sentencing, and ambition mutate instantly.
  • Memories can be copied once. Courts, grief, and black markets explode into existence.
  • AI can legally inherit property. Family structures, wealth transfer, and personhood law fracture.
  • Sleep is editable. Class systems form between enhanced and unenhanced workers.

Failure modes

Common mistakes that make science fiction feel hollow.

01

Worldbuilding without pressure

If the reader can admire your setting but never feel it squeezing someone, you built a museum, not a story.

02

Technology as decoration

If the same plot could happen in present-day Chicago with minimal changes, the premise is too superficial.

03

Rules with no tradeoffs

Every powerful system should create new dependency, inequality, temptation, or vulnerability.

04

Too many ideas at once

One premise explored deeply usually beats five premises touched lightly.

05

Philosophy with no plot

If characters only debate the idea without suffering through it, the story becomes an essay in costume.

06

Spectacle without meaning

Big visuals impress briefly. What lingers is the moral wound, the question, or the tragic choice.

Final check

The fast self-test before you draft.

Ask these before writing chapter one

  • Can I explain the premise in one sharp sentence?
  • Do the rules create tension instead of removing it?
  • Have I chased the consequences beyond the obvious?
  • Do I know who suffers most because of this world?
  • Does the premise generate the actual conflict?
  • Is there a meaningful cost at the ending?

The north star

Start with the change. Follow the consequences. Put a human being in the middle. Then force a choice no machine can solve for them.
